Breath of the Wild

Created on October 19, 2026

English | 中文

2026   ·   travel   ·   blog

I

While applying for my Australian visa, I learned that with a Chinese passport and an eligible Australian visa, I could fly from Australia to New Zealand on an NZeTA. I was already going all the way to Australia, so why not visit New Zealand while I was there? I decided to start in Queenstown, which I had heard described as a paradise for outdoor sports. Apparently, you could find just about any activity there.

The flight from Sydney to Queenstown took about three hours. We took off a little after four in the afternoon and reached New Zealand just in time for sunset. I happened to have a window seat, so I watched the Southern Alps stretch across the South Island below me. The last light fell on the snowy peaks, with bands of pink and magenta crossing the sky. As we came in to land, we caught the blue hour too. In a minute or two, the sky went from red to blue and finally black.

Queenstown Airport is about five kilometres from the centre of town. The town fans out around Lake Wakatipu in an L shape, and my hostel, Haka House Queenstown Lakefront, was at one end of the L, facing the lake. It was already ten at night when I got there. I opened the door to find a couple on one of the beds, fully clothed, I should add. They were both from North York in England and had been travelling the world for two years. They had made their way through several countries in Asia before coming to Australia and New Zealand, and planned to go back through Asia on their way home. They had already been to Beijing and wanted to visit Shanghai next.

The next morning, I hiked Queenstown Hill. It’s a beginner-friendly walk of about five kilometres return, with roughly 500 metres of climbing. My suitcase had no room for hiking shoes, so I went in my everyday shoes and felt my feet slide around in them all the way up. I passed quite a few other hikers and found myself guessing where they were from. A fair number struck me as possibly being of German descent. Another couple reached the summit at about the same time as I did, so I asked them to take a photo for me.

On the way down, I got a call saying my Shotover Jet booking had been cancelled because of the weather. I switched to the slightly gentler KJet for the afternoon. For lunch, I went to the famous Fergburger. The bakery, café and doughnut shop next door are all part of the Ferg family too, and I had already bought a croissant at the bakery that morning. The burger was huge, and there was something distinctive about the sauce. It was up there with the best burgers I’ve ever had. KJet turned out to be a jet boat that seated about twenty people and sped around Lake Wakatipu and the nearby waterways. Every so often it would spin 180 degrees in about a second, making sure everyone got equally wet.

That evening, I took the Skyline gondola up the hill to ride the Luge. There were two tracks, one for beginners and one for more experienced riders. The little carts picked up speed under gravity, and with a brake on the handlebars, you could even race a little. At the bottom, a short chairlift took you back to the start, much like at a ski resort. I had paid for six rides, but it started raining after my fourth, so I called it a day.

I had dinner at the Stratosfare buffet up on the hill. A buffet does feel a little awkward when you’re there alone. Back at the hostel, I told the British couple how I had spent the day. They said it sounded exhausting. For them, a day out usually meant getting something to eat, wandering around a bit and heading back.

II

The next day, I joined a day tour to Milford Sound. Once again, I was the only Chinese person in the group. I’d wanted to see a fiord for myself. Glaciers carved out deep valleys here, and when the ice retreated, the sea filled them in. Milford Sound looks fairly close to Queenstown on a map, but the road takes a long way around. With the rain and stops in a few small towns, it took us about six hours to get there.

At the harbour, we boarded a boat and sailed from the head of the fiord out towards the open sea. Travel guides say Milford Sound is at its best in the rain. Seen through the mist, the water pouring off the cliffs looks as though it’s falling straight from the sky. The rain stopped shortly after we set off, and the sun came out. There were still plenty of slender waterfalls on the rock faces, some with drops of over a hundred metres. I even spotted some penguins, just tiny shapes in the distance. It was a lovely surprise.

The following day, I took the shuttle to Wānaka, about an hour and a half from Queenstown. I had booked with Wanaka Bike Tours, who would drive me to the start of a mountain bike trail and let me ride back to town to return the bike. When I turned up, it turned out I was their only booking that day. My driver and guide was an older man with a van and a bike in the back. We talked about all sorts of things on the drive. I remember bits about the Golden Week crowds, how often he now saw visitors from China and India, how a house by Lake Wānaka could cost several million New Zealand dollars, and how to follow the route on my map. The starting point was beside Lake Hāwea. There was barely anyone around, and the blue lake stretched out in front of me. I asked him to take a photo of me with the bike.

Mountain biking felt completely different from road cycling. The trail was well maintained and mostly dirt or gravel. My bike only had front suspension, so I felt plenty of the bumps. The path followed a stream, took me past cows that stared back from behind a wire fence, and crossed wooden bridges built for bikes. There was also a road bridge only one lane wide, with a traffic light at each end to take turns letting cars across. I saw plenty of other mountain bikers, most of whom seemed to be locals. Back in Wānaka, I went to see #ThatWanakaTree, the one growing out of the lake that people call the loneliest tree in the world. The shore was full of people who had come to see it, just like me. Maybe it had never been lonely at all.

III

Auckland was the last stop of the trip. The views on the flight from Queenstown were just as lovely, though I hadn’t managed to get a window seat this time. New Zealand is still welcoming new immigrants, and walking through central Auckland, I got a sense of how many parts of the world people here had come from. Auckland is known as the City of Sails, and the city centre has a harbour on either side. The water had a distinctive colour. The closest match I could think of was Tiffany blue.

From my hostel, LyLo Auckland, I followed Queen Street right down to Queens Wharf. Moored at the waterfront was the Carnival Splendor, the very same ship I’d seen in Sydney five days earlier. What a coincidence! I took the ferry to Devonport and walked up Mount Victoria. The hill is less than a hundred metres high, and from the top I could see Auckland spread out around the Sky Tower, with neighbourhoods stretching in every direction and houses thinning out in the distance.

That evening I went out for steak. I had meant to try one in Queenstown, but the nicer restaurants all needed a booking, so I saved it for Auckland. I ordered a Wagyu ribeye, which turned out to be one of the best steaks I’ve had.

Back at the hostel, I took my laptop to the common area to catch up on the news. A woman from China was sitting on her own beside me. We got talking, and I learned she was on a working holiday visa. She had just arrived in New Zealand and was about to start looking for a job. Before this, she had already lived and worked in several other countries. I found that fascinating. Few people I know have lived that way.

The next morning, I had a look around the University of Auckland before packing up for the airport. I passed a bookshop on the way, browsed for a bit, and came out with a 2027 Lonely Planet travel guide. It might give me some ideas for my next trip. Who knows? It’s hard to take in how many places there are in the world and how many ways there are to live. Maybe before I settle down and decide what I want the rest of my life to look like, I want to find what I truly love and where I want to go.

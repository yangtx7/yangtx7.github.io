From Autumn to Spring

Created on October 12, 2026

English | 中文

2026   ·   travel   ·   blog

I

This trip did not get off to an easy start. It was not until mid-September that I learned I would have a fairly long holiday. It felt like a waste not to travel, but there was already very little time to prepare. I first looked into a Schengen visa, only to find that appointments for every country were booked until after China’s National Day holiday. Then I checked the Australian visa: I could apply online, and the processing time seemed manageable. I got moving, gathered the documents, and submitted my application just two days after hearing about the holiday. Then came the long wait. In the meantime, I even made a bet with my classmates about whether the visa would arrive quickly. If it did not, I would join them on a trip to Lake Baikal in Russia. In the end, the visa came through on the morning of the Mid-Autumn Festival.

I took the high-speed train to West Kowloon, then transferred to the Airport Express for Hong Kong International Airport. Apart from the HK$105 fare, the service was still excellent in every respect. It hardly felt like a railway that had been running for more than twenty years. The airport’s design had a futuristic feel: layers of white ramps, combined with the sparse crowds, gave it a faint air of melancholy. By comparison, I would be more inclined to call the glass-clad skyscrapers in mainland China modernist. I landed in Melbourne around noon, took the SkyBus to Southern Cross, the city’s transport hub, and tried Australia’s version of Burger King: Hungry Jack’s.

After leaving my luggage at Little Drifter Melbourne, I headed to State Library Victoria. Downtown Melbourne felt like a typical British or American city centre. There were few trees along the pavements I walked, and occasionally I passed someone experiencing homelessness. Outside the library, I happened upon an anti-Israel demonstration. A few hundred people were waving Palestinian flags in the square, while onlookers stopped to take photographs. The library’s British character was appealing, and both The Quad and The Dome left an impression. Yet the whole place seemed to have become one enormous photo spot, with considerably more tourists than readers. This was already near the end of China’s National Day holiday; I could hardly imagine how crowded it might have been a few days earlier.

I then followed the route I had planned around the CBD. It fell within the Free Tram Zone, and the trams were one of Melbourne’s distinctive features. Route 35 stood out: unlike the others, it used vintage-looking trams and made a loop around the city centre. I walked through Royal Arcade, Block Arcade, Centre Place, and finally Degraves Street. They reminded me a little of the shopping streets I had visited in Japan, with shops lining either side, except that here the streets had a British character and seemingly endless cafés.

Eventually, I reached Flinders Street to see the classical Flinders Street Station. Next door was Federation Square, which I had chosen as my final stop because the 2026 Melbourne International Games Week was running from October 2 to 11. I had hoped to look around some of the activities, but could not find the entrances and gave up. Having barely slept on the flight the night before, I was already exhausted. I abandoned my plan to continue to the Yarra River, went back to Chinatown for dinner, and returned to the hostel.

Melbourne’s reputation as a welcoming city for Chinese people made sense. Most of the Chinese people I encountered seemed to be international students; a smaller number were older people, whom I guessed might have come here to retire. I saw relatively few middle-aged people. Among the older people I met, those from mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan seemed roughly equal in number, perhaps with even fewer from the mainland. As for students, Chinatown occupied a fairly central part of the CBD, and groups of them were everywhere. Its shops, along with those in the nearby malls, felt like a copy of home: bubble tea, fried skewers, malatang, hotpot—almost every restaurant brand I could name seemed to be represented. Even food delivery felt familiar. There was Panda delivery, and while eating in a restaurant I could hear the familiar terminal announcement: “Your delivery order number xx has been automatically accepted.” It was hard to say whether this familiarity was a good thing or a bad one.

My second day began at Queen Victoria Market, where I wandered around and bought a Danish pastry for breakfast. From there, I walked through Carlton Gardens to the Royal Exhibition Building. The surroundings were lovely, with sights such as a mother duck leading her ducklings around. Next came Fitzroy. Outside downtown, the buildings gradually shrank to two or three storeys. GPT had recommended the neighbourhood for its street art and carefully curated boutiques. But as someone who tends to be rather tightly wound, I found it difficult to settle into such a chilled-out atmosphere, so I mostly just passed through.

In the afternoon, I went to the National Gallery of Victoria and arrived just as a free guided tour was about to begin. An older woman volunteering as a guide took about ten of us around a selection of exhibits. She had clearly prepared thoroughly. Afterwards, I visited the Shrine of Remembrance, which commemorates Australians who served in the First and Second World Wars and other conflicts. The displays were beautifully put together. Of the war-related museums I have visited, this had the most impressive curation, with a real sense of immersion. My last stop was the Royal Botanic Gardens, a beautiful park with all sorts of people stretched out on the lawns.

For my final day in Melbourne, I booked a Great Ocean Road day tour. Amusingly, I had only settled the itinerary a day or two before leaving, with GPT helping to plan most of the routes, and had ended up booking an English-speaking tour. I was the only Chinese person in the group. Once everyone arrived, we introduced ourselves and discovered that our group of a little over ten people represented seven or eight countries. Our guide and driver was a middle-aged man from New Zealand who drove while talking about the region’s history and everyday life. Unfortunately, my listening skills were not quite up to the task. I could follow him when I concentrated completely, but the moment my attention wandered, I lost track.

Still, it was a very enjoyable tour. We first took the inland road to the Twelve Apostles, then followed the Great Ocean Road back towards Melbourne, with a few short walks, two or three stops in country towns, and a chance to see wild kangaroos along the way. The Australian countryside reminded me of my impression of rural America: towns only a few hundred metres across, detached houses of different styles, pickup trucks parked beside the road, and a church, clinic, and small supermarket in the town centre.

II

I deliberately booked an early flight from Melbourne to Canberra, leaving the hostel at five in the morning, because I wanted to try the little Dash 8-400. It was powered by two turboprop engines, and I had been assigned a seat close to a propeller. A quick measurement on my Apple Watch put the cabin noise above 85 decibels. Despite the early hour, the flight was nearly full. The people around me looked like Australian civil servants or military personnel, with a bearing quite different from ordinary tourists.

Canberra was a fairly small city. Around the National Triangle were landmarks such as Parliament House, the National Gallery, and the National Library. I visited Parliament House first, the only attraction on this trip where I had to go through security. Inside were a meeting hall, the House of Representatives, and the Senate, along with portraits of former prime ministers and displays introducing the current members of parliament. On the roof was a large lawn, from which I could look along Canberra’s central axis. There were few tall buildings, and the streets were broad and quiet. Afterwards, I visited the National Gallery, whose displays covered a fairly wide range of art.

Next was Floriade, Canberra’s spring flower festival. I arrived on its second-to-last day. There seemed to be relatively few tourists and more local families out together. At around five, as the day was coming to a close, there was a dance performance, apparently featuring groups from local secondary schools.

My hotel was beside Canberra railway station. I had booked a train to Sydney departing at noon the next day, but checkout was at ten, so I spent the intervening time walking around the neighbourhood and getting a small taste of local life. The streets around the hotel were almost deserted at night, and the hotel seemed to assume that its guests would arrive by car. Fortunately, there was a bus stop nearby; otherwise, even getting there would have been a problem.

III

The train from Canberra to Sydney took almost four and a half hours, with phone reception coming and going along the way, before arriving at Sydney Central. Sydney’s public transport had a distinctive identity: trains, metro, light rail, buses, and ferries were clearly differentiated, each using its initial as a symbol. My hostel was in The Rocks, near Circular Quay. Once I reached the neighbourhood, I felt I understood the name: despite being beside the sea, it had considerable changes in elevation. The walk from the station to the hostel must have involved a climb of about thirty metres.

Circular Quay was right beside the CBD and served as a ferry hub, with the Harbour Bridge to the left and the Opera House to the right. By late afternoon, it was packed. Beside the CBD’s towers stood extensive stretches of low-rise British-style buildings, continuing towards the Queen Victoria Building and, farther south, Central Station. I had bought a ticket for that evening’s The Opera Hits at the Sydney Opera House, essentially a selection of classic opera excerpts. The cast’s performance was decent enough, but the interior of the Opera House was more memorable. Around the Joan Sutherland Theatre, there were views towards the city from different angles.

The hostels also deserve a mention. Both Little Drifter Melbourne and YHA Sydney Harbour were excellent. Their common areas felt larger than all the guest rooms combined, and both had enormous kitchens with more than ten cooking stations, plenty of refrigerators, and several large containers of utensils and knives. I was not quite ready to cook for myself in a hostel—perhaps that suits people staying longer in one place—but plenty of others did. Usually, they made something straightforward: pasta, salad, or a ready meal.

My second day in Sydney happened to be a Sunday. In the morning, I visited the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney. Walking east from Circular Quay brought me into a large stretch of parkland, ending by the water at Mrs Macquarie’s Chair. I had planned to walk across the Sydney Harbour Bridge afterwards, but the sun was too strong, so I gave up. Back at Circular Quay, I found a Burger King for breakfast, tried toast with maple syrup, and then took a ferry to Taronga Zoo.

The ferry crossed the harbour with the city’s landmarks in full view. Taronga Zoo was built on a hillside: after getting off the ferry, I could take a bus to the top and work my way down. One of its signature sights was the giraffe enclosure halfway up the hill, where the animals could be photographed with the Opera House and Harbour Bridge behind them.

In the afternoon, I took a bus to Bondi Beach, another Sydney landmark. The beach was nearly a kilometre long and, being a Sunday, full of people. Everyone seemed to be doing something different: painting graffiti, playing volleyball, taking photographs, sunbathing, surfing. After so long in China’s big cities, I found it difficult to slip into that relaxed atmosphere. Perhaps it was also because I had already filled my itinerary, leaving no room to lie on the sand for an afternoon. I had no group of friends to spend the day at the beach with, either. In any case, I did not have sunshine throughout the trip: when I later visited Manly Beach, it was drizzling, and the atmosphere was quite different.

After Bondi Beach, I set out along the coastal walk towards Coogee. The path was carefully designed, running across uneven stretches of rock, with handrails and barriers appearing wherever they were needed to reassure me that I was still going the right way. Along the route was Waverley Cemetery, an enormous burial ground. The gravestones recorded lives from the nineteenth century through to recent years, with messages of remembrance from family and friends below the dates.

In the Chinese cultural setting I grew up in, gravestones and cemeteries are often considered inauspicious, something to keep as far away as possible. This cemetery felt more like a connection to the past. It reminded me of playing Cities: Skylines 1 and 2, where building a cemetery near a residential area could actually increase the happiness of the people living nearby. By the time I reached an open football field beside the sea, it was almost six, and I had to think about catching a bus back to the city. That evening, I made a special trip to Chinatown for a bowl of Zhang Liang Malatang costing the equivalent of 90 RMB. That, too, was a rather unusual experience.

Later, I visited QVB, the Queen Victoria Building, an old British-style shopping centre. Together with the arcades I had visited in Melbourne, it felt less grand in scale than shopping malls in China. Its appeal lay in having its own character and atmosphere, with a somewhat different mix of shops. The centres I visited mostly sold clothing and cosmetics, alongside mid-range restaurants and cafés.

Finally, I took the T8 to the airport. The railway felt somewhere between a metro and an intercity system, with distinctive double-decker carriages and seats whose direction could be reversed.

Qantas also left a strong impression throughout this journey. From check-in counters to terminals, its logo seemed to be everywhere at the airports I passed through. Next stop: New Zealand.
